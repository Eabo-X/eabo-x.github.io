If AI can already set up a training script for me, why am I still willing to spend an afternoon tracing how a batch becomes a loss, and how that loss then changes the model’s parameters?

Measured by that day’s output, the afternoon is hard to justify. Others have already built more with AI, while I’m still reading code I could have handed to a tool. But over a longer horizon, I care about a different question: when the tools upgrade again and the tasks change, what different judgments will the understanding from that afternoon let me make?

This is also where I start in thinking about the Junior Crisis. It makes people rethink the first job, and it confronts those still in school with an earlier question: how much do the standards we use today to judge our own progress still overlap with the ability to create value in the future?

The Junior Crisis describes a phenomenon in some industries: demand for entry-level roles is shrinking, squeezing the path by which newcomers enter a profession and build experience.

Many professions used to offer a fairly clear path of growth. Newcomers started by writing first drafts, organizing materials and handling simple tickets, received feedback as they finished tasks, and gradually took on more complex responsibilities. The company got output; the newcomer got an income and experience.

AI makes some of these entry-level tasks easier. For a firm, work that once went to a newcomer is increasingly done by senior staff using AI. Even if newcomers learn to use the same tools, whether they get a chance still depends on whether the firm needs one more person.

In the US, the Stanford Digital Economy Lab’s August 2026 update found that, as of June that year, employment of 22–25-year-olds in highly AI-exposed occupations was about 19% lower than projected from the trend of their peers in less-exposed occupations, with the gap arising mainly through reduced hiring. The authors describe this as a descriptive gap; how much of it AI actually causes remains uncertain. Other markets may not show the same magnitude. [Stanford Digital Economy Lab](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)

What concerns me is that entry-level jobs carry the function of training the next generation of practitioners. The first time you train a model wrong, the first time you misread a client’s needs, the first time a mentor’s nudge reveals a flawed assumption: together these make up experience. AI amplifies the judgment a person already has; if that experience never gets the chance to form, there is little for it to amplify.

This is hard for any single firm to solve. Most of what newcomers learn in a first job is general skill they can take with them. Becker pointed out long ago that firms are unwilling to pay for such skills, because the returns to training leave with the worker; and if newcomers cannot share the cost by accepting lower wages, training will be undersupplied. AI lowers a newcomer’s short-term output value and sharpens this old problem: each firm, on its own, has a reason to hire one fewer junior, and in aggregate the whole industry ends up with fewer future seniors.

A mismatch is opening up between what students are optimizing and what the future market will value. It has two sources: the market is repricing skills, and school evaluation shapes where we spend our time. The two look independent, but they point to the same mechanism.

AI training has reached deep into professional work. Mercor’s public data catalog lists expert tasks in finance, law, consulting and other fields, along with expert-built grading rubrics, reference solutions and environments designed around real workflows. How professional knowledge is used, and what counts as a good result, are being turned into training material in ever finer detail. In other words, experts are writing “what counts as good” into rewards. [Mercor](https://www.mercor.com/apex/off-the-shelf-data/)

This makes me take one scenario seriously. If certain vertical tasks with clear reward functions can be done by AI, cheaply and at close to the level of leading humans, within a year or less, then the payoff of a learning plan that takes several years will shift dramatically midway.

A person’s ability can keep growing while its relative scarcity keeps falling. Standardized analysis that once took years of training to produce will be done by many more people with tools. Analysis will still be needed, but the return from merely producing it will fall as supply expands.

The market will also create new demand. When some things become cheap, small projects that were once unaffordable become worth doing. Which roles shrink and which opportunities grow depends on how demand responds, and on what people can contribute in new ways of working.

So I weigh “being able to master a skill in the future” together with “someone being willing to pay for that skill in the future”. When choosing what to learn, I need to estimate how the relationship between the two will change.

Running in parallel with market change is another set of forces: the evaluations we receive every day.

Courses, exams, degrees and internships give students clear targets. Solving a problem, moving up a ranking, earning a qualification: each usually comes with fairly clear feedback. In RL terms, this is a dense, immediate reward. With limited time, students putting effort into these targets is a response to existing incentives.

When I say school’s reward function is biased, I mean it tends to put clearer weight on performance that is easy to assess and easy to compare. An exploration that hasn’t yet produced a pretty result, a challenge to the problem itself, or a deep understanding of a real need often takes extra steps before anyone can see it. Real-world value, by contrast, is usually a sparse, delayed reward: you may not know for months whether a judgment was right, and by then it’s hard to say which step deserves the credit.

Training in mathematics, statistics and serious reading can sustain learning for a very long time. Still, the signal grades give about these foundations is some distance from all the information needed to create value in future work. An assignment already provides the question, the data and the grading criteria; real work often requires you to first decide whether the question is worth solving, whether the data is trustworthy, and what result would count as an improvement. In other words, homework hands you the reward; real work often asks you to define it first.

When the market’s returns to skills adjust faster while curricula and evaluation change on longer cycles, what students are working hard to optimize will gradually drift away from what the future market values more.

And the two are not independent. What school is best at rewarding are tasks with standard answers that are easy to grade; those are also the tasks easiest to write as rewards, and therefore the first ones AI learns. The expert rubrics and training environments mentioned above keep pushing the boundary of “easy to grade” outward. Optimizing for school’s reward is, to some extent, training yourself on the very distribution AI is being trained on.

So the human position is shifting: from optimizing a given reward toward defining and checking rewards — deciding which problems are worth solving, what counts as an improvement, and where an evaluation might be gamed.

The Junior Crisis also makes this mismatch harder to detect and correct. With fewer entry-level openings, students have fewer chances to meet real users, see how organizations actually run and get feedback on real work. It becomes harder for them to estimate what their abilities can actually contribute, and they lean more on visible signals like grades and CVs. The practical experience that could have helped people correct course becomes scarcer. It is like an agent that has lost access to its environment and can only keep optimizing a proxy reward on offline data: the more it optimizes, the harder the bias is to detect.

In this environment, I want to take more responsibility for judging how I spend my time. School evaluation, signals from the job market and the paths others have taken are all worth consulting. I also need to use my own experience to check how closely those signals relate to what I actually want to achieve. If the human position is shifting toward defining rewards, then defining a reward function for yourself is itself practice in that skill.

I separate two questions. One is how large a marginal gain AI gives me. The other is, once AI is universally available, how much my involvement can still improve the outcome.

I consider the second question alongside future demand, focusing on problems that will still be worth solving and where my involvement can improve the result. For example, AI can help a researcher write experiments and process data faster. If that researcher can pick a more explanatory question, spot a hole in the evaluation, or find an assumption that needs to change, their judgment will shape the value of the entire set of experiments. These judgments all happen before optimization begins: first decide what to optimize, then check whether the signal being optimized can be trusted.

I pay attention to places where a person’s concrete understanding and involvement still clearly change the outcome. Their value has to be tested in real tasks. Someone with “good judgment” should be able to say which decision their judgment changed, and what result it led to.

There is also an opportunity cost. Spending a hundred hours getting better at an operation that tools will soon make universal builds up something completely different from spending the same time understanding a long-standing problem in an industry. Before investing, I want my own view of that difference, and to keep revising it as new information comes in.

AI changes many of the conditions under which things get done, and with them the range over which some experience applies.

In the past, a piece of work may have needed many people because execution was expensive. A newcomer may have needed years before handling a project alone because gathering information, implementing and validating results were all costly. When these constraints change, old experience needs to be re-tested.

So I want to make more attempts that get real feedback. Before doing something, write down what I expect to happen and why. Afterwards, see where the results departed from my expectations, and ask which condition was at work.

My own experience needs the same treatment. A success may include luck; a failure may come from local execution. How far a piece of experience generalizes needs to keep being checked under different conditions.

For me, that is exactly the value of first-hand experience: it gives my judgments about markets, technology and people a chance to be corrected by reality.

Even when AI can produce results quickly, I still set aside some time for things that look a bit “dumb”.

For example, reading a model’s low-level code: following a batch to see how data enters the model, how the loss is computed and how gradients change the parameters. Or following an inference request to understand how prefill, the KV cache and decode relate, then changing the context length and watching how memory and latency respond.

You can start from one critical path and gradually understand how it works. One way to test that understanding is to predict the result before changing a condition, then run the experiment and see what you missed.

I learned this the hard way in my scaling-law experiments. At a fixed compute budget, loss as a function of model size N traces a U-shaped curve, and the minimum corresponds to the D/N ratio that gives the lowest loss. But you don’t know that ratio in advance, so the sampling range has to start as a guess. Several times I set D/N too high, the models were all too small, not a single point landed on the right half of the U, and the minimum could only be guessed by extrapolation. The problem wasn’t the fit; it was my default assumption that the minimum would fall inside the range I had drawn. The sampling range is itself an assumption that needs testing first.

Post-training was similar. Batch size, sequence length and learning rate jointly determine the size and noise of each weight update. Change the batch size or sequence length, and the tokens seen per step, the total number of steps and the gradient noise all change with it, so a learning rate that used to work may no longer fit. Hyperparameters tuned under one setting need re-testing when the conditions change, which is exactly what I mean by accumulating experience together with the conditions it holds under.

Similar investments can happen outside technology. When reading an empirical paper, reproduce the key results yourself and check the assumptions the conclusions rest on. When building a product, walk with a user through their existing workflow end to end, and watch where they hesitate and why they still use a method that looks inefficient.

Learning during delivery deserves separate attention. In a randomized controlled trial published in January 2026, Anthropic had 52 participants, mostly junior engineers, learn an unfamiliar programming library and then take an independent quiz. The AI-assisted group averaged 50%; the hand-coding group averaged 67%. Within the AI group, those who used AI to ask conceptual questions scored above 65%, while those who handed off code generation entirely scored below 40%: the difference lies not just in whether you use the tool, but in how. These results reflect understanding right after the task, and they make me pay closer attention to which parts of the thinking I actually take part in when using tools. [anthropic.com](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)

These investments help me understand later tools and problems faster, and leave room for future choices. Part of the reason is also simple: I genuinely want to know why it works the way it does. My own reward function can leave room for that curiosity.

AI lets individuals do more, and also makes many practices easier to spread.

If an advantage comes mainly from using the same model or mastering an easily copied set of operations, others will soon catch up. The next generation of models may also turn work that needs skill today into a default feature. Even as individual output rises dramatically, sustained excess returns still face competition.

So I’m skeptical of the “super-individual” idea: that general-purpose AI tools alone can let one person hold an advantage for long. Once someone can build the technology, they still need to understand demand, earn trust, and work with others to keep things running.

This is also why I value connections between people.

Imagine several people discussing the same AI customer-service product. The engineer may focus on answer quality; the shop owner cares about refunds and cash flow; the support lead cares about who takes over when something goes wrong; a customer hopes that when there’s trouble, they can find someone willing to take responsibility. After hearing these different concerns, our judgment that “this product is done” may change.

I hope to talk more with people of different ages, professions and views. We can discuss technology, and also what stability, fairness and success mean. Behind each person’s judgments there may be constraints and costs I have not yet experienced.

Long-term conversation also builds trust, which makes people willing to share real difficulties, give feedback and try something together. These relationships also shape what I want to do. After understanding more people’s lives, I may reconsider which problems are worth my time.

The future I hope to see is one where students encounter complete problems earlier, with enough guidance and feedback.

Such opportunities require someone to supply the problems, invest mentoring time and pay the cost of the learning period. This brings us back to the old problem: the returns to training spill over as people move, so no single firm has much reason to bear that cost on its own. Paid projects and apprenticeships matter precisely because they find someone to pay it. Whether the Junior Crisis eases depends in large part on how these opportunities are organized.

Individuals also need to keep checking their own reward functions. A failed experiment that helps me find a flaw in my original judgment should count as progress. Reading that has nothing to show for now, if it helps me understand a problem I later keep running into, can also be a worthwhile investment. Market feedback, my own interests and the impact I hope to have can all feed into this evaluation.

If I measured an afternoon of reading code only by how much I shipped that day, I would quickly lose patience. The reward from that afternoon is sparse and delayed; its value often shows up months later, and by then it’s hard to say which afternoon made the difference. But if those hours let me predict the consequences of a change or find a question worth pursuing, I’m willing to make time for them.

When models get stronger again, I will reassess whether this is still worth doing. The environment changes, and so does the reward; a choice that makes sense today may not make sense tomorrow. I hope I will always be able to explain the choice, and be willing to change my answer in the face of new experience.
